Our research

We study how different animals have adapted to cope with viral infections. While mammals, reptiles, and other vertebrates share a similar set of "building blocks" of immune genes and protein domains, these blocks have been uniquely assembled and modified over evolutionary time in different species to protect the host. We use genetic screens, functional genomics, and classic virology to uncover these mechanisms as ways to learn about evolution, virology, and molecular biology.

Read more

Our research is guided by three overarching questions: (1) What immune defenses have evolved across diverged vertebrate lineages? (2) How do gene duplications, domain rearrangements, and rapid evolution give rise to new immune functions? And (3) How do viruses evade immune defenses across different species? These questions drive a discovery-based research program in which we follow our curiosity into new models and systems.

Genetic Screens
Our lab is tapping into the deep reservoirs of immune diversity that exist in nature. Inspired by classic cDNA screens, we perform function-first screens of cDNAs from diverged species to identify antiviral proteins encoded by their genomes. Other screening modalities, such as CRISPR knockout screens and parallel ISG ectopic expression screens complement this approach. While we are maintaining our focus on squamate reptiles as a diverse lineage with powerful immune innovations, we are expanding our screens to birds, amphibians, and beyond.

Genomics and Evolutionary Genetics
We additionally use genomics and genetics to nominate genes of interest. Unique domain organizations exist in diverged vertebrates, as do massive, lineage-specific expansions of known immune-involved protein families. Signatures of rapid evolution – a hallmark of genes that are involved in immune defenses – also pinpoint potential antivirals. We make use of modern sequencing technologies to generate genomic resources, such as the first assembly of the Iguana genome, to the broader research community. We are actively working on a reference quality genome annotation for the Iguana and are happy to share our draft annotations with interested researchers upon request.

Lab members

Ian Boys

Principal Investigator

Ph.D. UT Southwestern Medical Center.

Read more

Ian is a virologist and evolutionary geneticist who studies immune diversity to uncover new ways to block viral infection. He studied bacteriophages as an undergrad at Baylor University and antiviral immunity in bats as a grad student at UTSW. As a postdoc in the Department of Human Genetics at the University of Utah, he studied molecular mimicry/anti-immune mechanisms in poxviruses and antiviral immunity in lizards.

Outside of the lab, Ian splits his time between the outdoors and being a nerd. He enjoys hiking, kayaking (technically packrafting), camping, cycling, and running, as well as board games and MTG. He's also an amateur photographer, a relapsing cellist, and an avid cook. Much of his free time is spent with his wife and two cats.
